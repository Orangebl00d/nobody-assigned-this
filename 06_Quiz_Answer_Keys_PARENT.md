# Answer Keys (Parent / Mentor)

Go over answers together after each quiz. When the student picks a different answer and defends it well, give credit. The point is the reasoning.

---

## Session 3 Exercise: Forgiveness or Permission? (suggested answers)

| # | Situation | Call | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fixing typos on the club site, already has edit access | **Just do it (Level 5)** and tell the club lead | Within their authority, low downside, easy to undo |
| 2 | Posting a friend's wipeout video for views | **Permission, and probably don't** | Someone else's reputation; the upside is only yours |
| 3 | Emailing an author with one question | **Go** (after a parent review) | Worst case is silence |
| 4 | Rearranging the living room while parents are out | **Permission** | Shared space, other people's stuff |
| 5 | Lunchtime chess meetup in the library | **Permission for the space, forgiveness for the idea** | The librarian controls the room; the meetup itself needs no one's blessing |
| 6 | $15 domain on a parent's card | **Permission, always** | Money |
| 7 | Public comment at town council about a crosswalk | **Go**, and tell a parent | Public comment exists for this; low downside, real upside |
| 8 | Answering a reporter on behalf of the club | **Permission** | Speaking for a group, reputation at stake |

## Quiz 1 Key

**1. c.** Watch for **d** as a tempting trap. Asking for help is often the most agentic move available. Agency means you own finding the way, and asking is one of the ways.

**2. b.** It's fear of judgment. Mack's move is to name the actual 1–7 people. "Everyone at school" usually turns into three specific classmates, and the fear shrinks. Option (d) deserves discussion: sometimes caution is realistic. Ask what the actual downside is.

**3. Sample answers:**
- **Level 2:** "Jordan hasn't done their part. I think they're sick or checked out."
- **Level 5:** "Jordan's been unreachable since Monday. I split up the remaining work: I built their slides from our shared notes so we're not stuck. I emailed Ms. Park to let her know how the work was divided, so grading is fair, and I texted Jordan that the slides are covered and asked if they can still present the section they researched."

Full credit requires telling the teacher (or explaining why not). A Level 5 that quietly covers for Jordan without telling anyone is well-meant but skips fairness and transparency. A strong answer also leaves Jordan a way back in.

**4. c.** It has a fact, a cause, options, a pick with a reason, and a specific ask with a date. It isn't Level 5 because the coach controls practice.

**5. c.** Other people's images on a public account need their OK. Option (a) is fine with a parent review. Option (b) is within the student's authority. Option (d) is low risk.

**6. c.** No reason why *this* person, and "mentor" plus "pick your brain" is a big, vague ask. Age (b) is fine, even helpful. Exclamation points (d) are a small issue, not the main one.

**7. b.** The student acted before being asked, did it well, and got recognized after. Options (a) and (d) are "taking" at others' expense. That's main-character mode.

**8. Short answer rubric (4 points):**
- 1 pt: A real problem with at least one specific fact
- 1 pt: A plausible cause and 2–3 genuine options
- 1 pt: A clear pick with a reason (no "idk, what do you think?")
- 1 pt: A specific ask and a date, addressed to someone who can actually say yes

## Quiz 2 Key

**1. b.** The student decided the problem, the audience, and the requirements. AI built it. Option (a) hands the deciding to AI. Option (c) confuses effort with agency. Option (d) is Graham's dead code.

**2. c.** Good follow-up question: "Where in your own week does this happen?"

**3. b.** Specific decisions, including one rejection. Option (c) is dishonest if AI was used. Option (d) is vague; ask "which decisions were yours?"

**4. c.** Agency includes ethics. The best answer holds the line and offers a better path. Option (b) is defensible, but reporting isn't required to pass. Discuss.

**5. c.** One follow-up is normal. Option (a) is annoying. Option (d) is invasive, and the student shouldn't be hunting down personal numbers at all.

**6. b.** "Not this" is a gift: they told the student exactly what they need. Bonus discussion: this is how many real businesses find their first product.

**7. b.** Small, safe, reversible, and aimed at learning. Option (a): an apology is a thing you don't ship ugly. Option (c) harms someone else. Option (d): safety isn't a "fix it later" item.

**8. b.** Two people have said stop. That's main-character mode. Option (a) is fine because they told people.

**9. Sample answer:**
> "I built the slides with embedded videos that needed Wi-Fi and didn't test them in the classroom first. That cost our group about three minutes, and we lost points for time. I've already reuploaded the videos as files on a USB drive and sent the group a working version. Next time I'll test on the actual equipment the day before."

Rubric (4 points): owns the action ("I built… I didn't test"), names the impact, gives a fix, gives prevention. The Wi-Fi being bad can be true and mentioned, but it can't be the whole answer.

**10. Short answer rubric (3 points):**
- 1 pt: Names a specific moment and which triangle belief it was
- 1 pt: Honest about what they actually did, including if they stalled
- 1 pt: A concrete, believable plan for next time
